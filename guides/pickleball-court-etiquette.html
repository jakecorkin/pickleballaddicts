Etiquette 101

Pickleball Court Etiquette

Nobody hands you a rulebook for this part. Here's how the paddle queue works, who makes the calls, and the unwritten rules that keep open play running smoothly.

The official rulebook covers serving, the kitchen, and scoring — but it doesn't tell you how to get on a court in the first place, or what to do when a stray ball rolls onto your court mid-rally. Most of what makes open play feel welcoming (or awkward) comes down to a set of habits regulars pick up over time and newcomers have to guess at. Here's what to know before your first session at a public court.

How the Paddle Queue Actually Works

At busy public courts, there's rarely a sign-up sheet — instead, players use a paddle queue to decide who's up next. Some facilities have a "paddle saddle," a rack of tubes that hold paddles in groups of four, with a sliding marker showing which group is up. Just as often, it's simply a line of paddles propped against the fence or stacked on a bench near the court: when your group finishes, you add your paddle to the back of the line, and the next four paddles in line take the court. Write your name on your paddle — it's a small habit that saves a lot of "whose paddle is this?" confusion.

On busier days, some courts run two lines instead of one: a "winners" line and a "losers" line, so the group that just won plays the next group of winners while the group that just lost plays the next group waiting. Over the course of a session, this naturally sorts players into games of a similar skill level without anyone having to referee it.

Whatever system a court uses, the unwritten rule is the same: don't jump the line, and don't invite a friend to cut ahead by rearranging paddles so they can play with you sooner. If you showed up together, get in line together.

Line Calls Are Yours to Make — and Only Yours

Each side is responsible for making calls on their own half of the court. If you see your own shot land out, call it out on yourself — that's expected, not optional. Only call a ball "out" on your opponent's side if you're genuinely certain, and make the call loudly and immediately, before the next shot is played. If you disagree with an opponent's call, you can question it, but do so respectfully rather than arguing; in recreational play without a referee, their call on their side of the net stands.

It's also standard to call out the score before every serve, loud enough for everyone on the court to hear. It keeps both teams honest and avoids the awkward mid-point argument about what the score actually was.

Ball Safety Between Courts

At most facilities, courts sit close together, and balls stray onto neighboring courts constantly. If a ball rolls onto your court while play is happening, the safety rule takes priority over the game in progress: call out "ball!" loudly so the players on that court know something's coming, then retrieve and return it once it's safe. If your own ball rolls onto someone else's court, make eye contact with a player there and roll or toss it back rather than walking across an active game to grab it. If you need to walk behind or across a court that's still playing, wait until the point ends first.

Basic Sportsmanship

None of this is complicated, but it's easy to forget in the moment: introduce yourself to players you don't know before the game starts, and meet everyone at the net afterward to shake hands or tap paddles. Skip the trash talk, and never throw your paddle or smash a ball in frustration — it happens, but it's one of the fastest ways to make a court feel tense. If you're clearly the stronger player in a casual, mixed-level game, ease off rather than hitting to actively embarrass a beginner; it's a rec game, not a tournament.

Don't Sandbag

"Sandbagging" — deliberately rating yourself lower than you actually play so you can dominate an easier group or bracket — is one of the most consistently called-out etiquette violations in the sport, and it's explicitly against the rules at sanctioned tournaments. On a casual court it's less formal but just as unwelcome: a player who's clearly a strong 4.0 camping out in a 3.0 group isn't helping anyone improve, and it usually gets noticed fast. Rating systems like DUPR, which calculate your number from actual match results rather than self-assessment, make sandbagging harder to pull off than it used to be — but the simplest fix is just to rate yourself honestly and look for games near your real level. See our guide to pickleball ratings for more on how DUPR and self-ratings work.

When in Doubt, Watch First

Every court has its own local rhythm — how the paddle queue is organized, whether games are win-by-two to 11 or something shorter for busy days, how strict the line-call culture is. If you're new to a facility, it's worth watching one game before jumping in the line. Five minutes of observation will usually answer more questions than asking around, and it signals to the regulars that you're paying attention to how their court runs.

Key Takeaways

  • Most public courts run on a paddle queue (a rack or a simple line) — add your paddle to the back and wait your turn; don't cut ahead.
  • Make calls honestly on your own side; call your score out loud before every serve.
  • Call "ball!" immediately if a stray ball rolls onto an active court, and wait for a point to finish before crossing a court.
  • Ease up on beginners in casual mixed-level games rather than playing to embarrass them.
  • Don't sandbag — rate yourself honestly rather than parking in a group below your real level.
