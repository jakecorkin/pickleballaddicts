Etiquette 101

Pickleball Court Etiquette: Unwritten Rules Every Beginner Should Know

None of this is in the official rulebook, but showing up to open play without knowing it is the fastest way to get side-eyed by regulars. Here's how to fit in from your first session.

Pickleball's official rulebook covers serving, faults, and scoring — but most of what makes open play run smoothly isn't written down anywhere. It's a set of habits regulars pick up over dozens of sessions: how the paddle stack works, who calls a ball "out," what to do when a stray ball rolls onto your court mid-rally. Skip them and you're not breaking a rule, exactly, but you'll be the person everyone quietly avoids getting paired with. Here's what to know before your first drop-in.

Learn the Paddle Stack Before You Play

Most public courts and rec centers run open play on a paddle rotation instead of a sign-up sheet. When you arrive, look for a rack, fence rail, or line painted on the ground near the court — that's where paddles go to mark your place in line. Set your paddle down in the order you arrived, and if you came with a group who wants to play together, stack your paddles on top of each other so the next group knows you're a unit, not four separate singles.

The standard flow is: the group whose paddles are up next takes the court, winners often stay on (house rules vary — ask if you're not sure), losers rotate off, and the next paddles in the stack rotate in. Never skip the stack or wave your way onto an open court without checking it first — jumping the line is one of the fastest ways to annoy an entire group of regulars.

Line Calls Are Yours to Make — And Yours Alone

Whoever is standing on the side of the court where the ball lands makes the call on that side, "in" or "out" — not the opposing team, and not a bystander, unless someone specifically asks for a second opinion. If you didn't clearly see space between the ball and the line, the convention is to call it in; you should only call a ball "out" when you're genuinely certain. Make the call immediately, right after the bounce and before your opponent's next shot — a late "out" call, especially one that comes after you realize you're about to lose the point, reads as a gamed call even if it wasn't.

When you're genuinely unsure, the accepted etiquette is to resolve it in your opponent's favor and just replay the point if it really can't be settled. It feels like giving something up in the moment, but it's the norm that keeps casual games from turning into arguments.

Stray Balls: Yell, Stop, Don't Chase

Courts are usually packed close together, so balls end up on the wrong court constantly — it's not a big deal as long as you handle it the right way. If a ball from another court rolls or bounces onto yours mid-point, stop your rally immediately; the point gets replayed once the ball is cleared, rather than played through. If your own shot goes stray toward a neighboring court, call out "ball!" loudly so the players there know something is coming, then wait for a break in their play before retrieving it — don't jog onto an active court mid-rally to grab it, even if it's just sitting there.

Match Your Intensity to Who You're Playing

If you're a stronger player matched up against clear beginners in open play, the expectation is to dial it back — work your soft game, keep points going, and skip the overhead smash at someone who's still learning to track the ball. It's not about letting people win; it's about not turning a casual rec session into batting practice at someone's expense. On the flip side, if you're new, it's fine to ask a local (or just watch a game first) which courts tend to run beginner-friendly versus more competitive, rather than assuming every open court is fair game.

Watch Your Path Between Courts

Courts at most facilities sit close together with shared walking lanes behind the baselines. If you need to cross behind a court that's mid-point, stop and wait — don't walk through someone's sightline or backswing while the ball's live. Once play pauses between points, cross quickly and keep moving; lingering behind an active court to watch is its own minor annoyance.

Call the Score, Every Time

Before every serve, say the score out loud — the server's score, the receiver's score, and (in doubles) which server you are, first or second. It feels unnecessary once you and your regular group know each other, but it keeps disputes from happening later in the game and it's expected even in the most casual open play. If you're new and the score-calling format still feels awkward, that's completely normal — it clicks after a handful of games.

Start and End Every Game the Same Way

If you don't already know the people you're about to play with, introduce yourself before the first serve — open play is as much a social scene as a competitive one, and most regulars appreciate a quick "hi, I'm [name]" more than you'd expect. At the end of the game, it's customary to meet at the net for a paddle tap (or handshake) with everyone on the court, win or lose. Celebrate a good win, but keep it modest — a fist pump or paddle tap is fine; running victory laps in front of the group you just beat isn't.

Key Takeaways

  • Check the paddle stack before taking a court — never skip the line, even if a court looks open.
  • Only the players on the side where the ball lands make the "in" or "out" call, made immediately after the bounce.
  • A stray ball on your court stops the rally for a replay; yell "ball!" and wait for a break in play before retrieving your own.
  • Stronger players should dial back power against clear beginners rather than playing to win big.
  • Call the full score before every serve, and meet at the net for a paddle tap after every game.
