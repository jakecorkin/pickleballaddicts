The Third Shot Drop, Step by Step

The single shot that separates players who get stuck at the baseline from players who reliably take over the net. Here's the stance, swing, and common mistakes, broken down.

Every rally that starts with a serve follows the same pattern for the serving team: serve, opponent returns, and now it's the serving team's third shot. Because of the two-bounce rule, that return has to bounce before anyone can volley it — which means the serving team is standing near the baseline while their opponents are already camped at the net. The third shot drop is the answer to that gap: a soft, arcing shot that lands in the opponents' kitchen instead of giving them something to attack, buying the serving team time to move up and even out the court position. It's widely considered the single hardest shot for players to become consistent with, and the one that most reliably separates a 3.0 game from a 3.5 or 4.0 one.

Why This Shot Exists

The area between the baseline and the kitchen line is sometimes called the "transition zone" or "no man's land" — and it's a bad place to get stuck. A ball hit hard at your feet while you're back there is nearly impossible to handle cleanly. A good third shot drop is what lets the serving team avoid lingering in that zone: drop it softly into the kitchen, take a couple of controlled steps forward while it's in the air, and arrive at the net on more even footing with the returning team. A weak or missed third shot, on the other hand, is exactly what creates those stuck-in-no-man's-land points.

The Setup: Stance and Positioning

Get your feet moving early. The goal is to be set up behind the ball with your paddle back and ready, rather than reaching for it at the last second — reaching is one of the most common sources of a mishit drop. As the ball approaches, bend your knees and get your weight forward onto the balls of your feet, not back on your heels.

Contact should happen out in front of your body. If you find yourself contacting the ball even with or behind your torso, you're almost always going to muscle it with your arm instead of guiding it with your shoulder — and that's where consistency falls apart.

The Swing: Short, Low-to-High, Shoulder-Driven

Keep the backswing short. A big backswing is one of the most common mistakes beginners make on this shot — it adds power and speed you don't want and makes the timing much harder to repeat. Take the paddle back low, below the height you expect to contact the ball, then swing up and through it in one smooth motion.

A common coaching cue is to keep your wrist and elbow locked and let your shoulder do the work, the same way you would on a dink. It feels counterintuitive — a "soft" shot seems like it should come from a loose, relaxed arm — but a floppy wrist is actually what makes the shot inconsistent, because it adds a variable joint that's hard to control under pressure. A stable, shoulder-driven motion repeats the same way every time, which is exactly what you want on a shot you're going to hit dozens of times a match.

Follow through toward your target and let the paddle finish high — around shoulder height or above. A follow-through that stops short or stays low tends to produce a flatter shot that either clips the net or sits up as an easy attack for your opponents.

Paddle Face Angle

The paddle face should be slightly open — angled up toward the sky — at contact, and the lower the ball is when you hit it, the more open the face needs to be to get it back over the net softly. As a general rule of thumb: a closed paddle face drives the ball, an open face drops it. If your drops are consistently popping up into an easy put-away for your opponents, an over-opened face (or making contact too late, after the ball has dropped past the ideal point) is usually the cause.

Where to Aim

Aim for the middle of the kitchen, biased slightly toward the center of the court rather than the sidelines. It's tempting to go for a tight angle near the line for a more aggressive shot, but the added difficulty isn't worth it while you're still building consistency — a third shot drop that reliably lands anywhere in the kitchen is far more valuable than an occasional highlight-reel one that misses more often than it lands.

Common Mistakes to Watch For

  • Big backswing: adds speed and timing risk you don't need on a shot that's supposed to be soft and controlled.
  • Loose wrist: feels natural for a "soft" shot but actually makes contact inconsistent — lock the wrist and elbow and let the shoulder guide the paddle instead.
  • Bobbing upper body: letting your chest rise as you swing tends to lift the paddle with it, which is a common cause of unwanted pop-ups.
  • Weight moving backward at contact: steepens your paddle angle and tends to lift the ball up rather than pushing it forward and down into the kitchen.
  • Short, low follow-through: usually means the ball comes out flatter than intended, either clipping the net or landing high enough to be attacked.

Drop, Drive, or Lob? Reading the Situation

The third shot drop isn't the only option on that shot — it's just the default when the situation calls for patience rather than pace.

  • Drop: your go-to when the return is deep, low, or otherwise hard to attack. It buys time and lets your team advance to the net instead of getting rushed.
  • Drive: a flat, firm shot aimed at your opponents' feet or body, best used when the return sits up high or short enough that you can attack it and put immediate pressure on the other team.
  • Lob: a rarer, high, deep shot best saved for opponents who are crowding the kitchen line hard and clearly leaning in, expecting a drop. Used sparingly and disguised well, it can catch an aggressive net-rusher out of position — but it's a low-percentage surprise weapon, not a shot to lean on regularly.

The short version: read the ball you're given. A tough, low, or deep return calls for a drop. A short or high one is a green light to drive. Everything else is a judgment call that gets easier with reps.

A Simple Drill to Build It

Start at the kitchen line with a practice partner, both of you hitting soft dinks back and forth to warm up the same low-to-high motion. Once you're both comfortable, have one player stay at the line while the other takes a single step back into the transition zone and keeps dropping the ball into the kitchen from there. Every few successful reps, take another step back — toward the baseline — while your partner stays at the line and keeps feeding. If your drops start missing consistently, take a step forward again until you rebuild control, then work your way back. Doing this a few minutes at a time, a couple of times a week, builds the touch and repeatable motion far faster than trying to fix the shot only in the middle of real points.

Key Takeaways

  • The third shot drop is a soft shot on the serving team's third shot of the rally, meant to land in the opponents' kitchen and avoid getting stuck in the transition zone.
  • Keep the backswing short, lock your wrist and elbow, and let your shoulder drive a low-to-high swing — a loose wrist makes the shot less consistent, not softer.
  • Paddle face should be slightly open at contact; the lower the ball, the more open the face needs to be.
  • Aim for the middle of the kitchen rather than tight sideline angles while you're building consistency.
  • Drop when the return is deep or hard to attack; drive when it's short or high; save the lob for opponents crowding the net.
