Scoring 101

Pickleball Scoring Explained

Side-out scoring, rally scoring, and the doubles quirks that trip up even experienced players — how the numbers actually work and why they're called out the way they are.

"What's the score?" is one of the most common questions on any pickleball court — and it's not because players are bad at math. Pickleball scoring has real quirks: only one side can score at a time (most of the time), doubles games start on an unusual number, and a growing number of tournaments now use a completely different system that changes several of those rules at once. Here's how it all actually works, from the basic 11-point game up through the newer rally-scoring format you may run into at a tournament or pro event.

Side-Out Scoring: The Default System

Side-out scoring is the traditional method, and it's still the official scoring system for the vast majority of pickleball — recreational play, most leagues, and USA Pickleball's own marquee events like the Golden Ticket series and the National Championships. The core rule is simple: only the serving side can score a point. If the receiving side wins the rally, no point is awarded to anyone — instead, the serve simply passes over (a "side out"), and the new serving side gets a chance to start scoring.

Games are typically played to 11 points, win by 2 (tournament formats sometimes use 15 or 21, still win by 2). Because only the server scores, a side-out scoring game can run long if both teams are evenly matched and trading serves without converting points — that back-and-forth is part of what makes side-out pickleball feel different from most other racket sports.

Positioning is tied to the server's own score, not the opponent's: the serve is always hit diagonally from the right-hand court when the server's score is even, and from the left-hand court when it's odd. That's true in both singles and doubles. It also doubles as a built-in error check — if the server is standing on the wrong side for their score, something in the rotation went wrong.

Calling the Score: Singles vs. Doubles

In singles, the score call is just two numbers: the server's score, then the receiver's score (for example, "4-2"). Simple, because there's only ever one server per side.

In doubles, there's a third number, because either player on the serving team can be the one currently serving. The call goes serving team's score, receiving team's score, then the server number (1 or 2) — for example, "5-3-2" means the serving team has 5, the receiving team has 3, and the second of the two partners is currently serving. Under USA Pickleball rules, the server has to call this out loud before every serve; starting the serve before announcing the score is itself a fault.

Why Doubles Starts at 0-0-2

This is the single most-asked scoring question for new doubles players. In a normal doubles sequence, both partners on a team get to serve — one loses the rally and passes to their partner, who serves next, and only after that second partner also loses does the serve go over to the other team. But at the very start of a doubles game, the first serving team only gets one server before the serve passes over, not two.

That's why the game opens with the score called as "0-0-2" instead of "0-0-1": by convention, that first server is labeled server number 2, which is really just a shorthand for "this team only gets this one turn." The rule exists to offset the natural advantage of serving first — without it, the team that starts serving would effectively get an extra possession before their opponents ever touch the ball.

After that opening exception, scoring settles into the normal pattern for the rest of the game: each side-out passes the serve to whichever player on the new serving team is standing in the right-hand (even) court, that player serves as "server 1," and if they lose the rally it passes to their partner as "server 2" before finally going back to the other team.

Singles Scoring Is More Straightforward

Singles sidesteps the whole server-number issue, since there's only ever one player per side. The server still serves from the right court when their own score is even and the left when it's odd, and they switch sides every time they win a point and serve again — the same position rule as doubles, just without a partner to hand the serve off to. If the server loses the rally, it's an immediate side out to the other player, no second-server step involved.

Rally Scoring: The Newer Alternative

Rally scoring flips the core rule of side-out scoring: a point is awarded on every single rally, regardless of who served. If the receiving side wins the point, they score and take over the serve — nobody just "passes" the serve without points changing anymore. Games under rally scoring are commonly played to 15 or 21 (instead of 11), always win by 2, since points come faster than in side-out play.

A few other things change along with it. The score call drops to two numbers even in doubles — your team's score, then the opponent's — since each server only gets one serve before the ball (and possibly the point) moves on, removing the need to track a server number. Positioning also works differently: instead of switching which side of the court you stand on after scoring, players generally stay put on one side for the whole game, and which partner serves is determined by whether the team's score is even or odd.

Rally scoring isn't the default anywhere in recreational play, and it isn't fully standardized yet even at the competitive level. As of the 2026 season, USA Pickleball has kept rally scoring as a provisional option that tournament directors can choose to offer (outside of its own Golden Ticket and National Championship events, which stick with side-out scoring), while it continues studying whether to make the format permanent. Pro tours have split on it: the PPA Tour offers rally scoring as a provisional option, Major League Pickleball reverted to traditional side-out scoring in 2026 after using rally scoring at its launch, and the APP Tour has stayed with side-out scoring throughout. Translation for rec players: you're very unlikely to run into rally scoring on a normal Tuesday-night court, but don't be surprised if a local tournament offers it as an option — check the format before you sign up.

Common Scoring Mistakes

  • Forgetting to call the score before serving — required on every serve, and technically a fault if skipped.
  • Assuming the receiving side can score — true under rally scoring, but not under standard side-out scoring, where only the server can.
  • Mixing up which team's score comes first — it's always the serving side's score first, not a fixed "home team" or higher score first.
  • Losing track of the server number in doubles — especially right after a side-out, when it resets and a new player becomes "server 1."
  • Applying side-out habits to a rally-scoring game — remembering to stay on one side rather than switching after scoring, and dropping the third number when calling score in doubles.

Key Takeaways

  • Side-out scoring (the default almost everywhere) only lets the serving side score; games are typically to 11, win by 2.
  • Score calls are two numbers in singles, three in doubles (server score, receiver score, server number 1 or 2).
  • Doubles games start at "0-0-2" because the first serving team only gets one server before the first side-out, offsetting the advantage of serving first.
  • Rally scoring awards a point on every rally to whichever side wins it, uses a two-number call even in doubles, and is still a provisional option at the tournament level, not the rec-play default.
  • Position is always tied to the server's own score — right court on even, left on odd — in both scoring systems.
